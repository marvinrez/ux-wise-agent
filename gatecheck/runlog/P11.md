No, and the part worth examining is not the number. It is that you are using a
half-remembered finding about memory to settle a question about navigation,
which is a question about findability.

Miller's work was about how much a person can hold for immediate recall. A
navigation menu asks nobody to recall anything. It is on the screen. The user
reads it, scans it, and leaves. Applying a recall limit to a visible list is a
category error, and a common one, which is why the figure gets quoted as a
design rule it was never about. Treat the popular version of it as contested
rather than as a limit you have to respect.

So the twelve items are not the problem, and they are not the absolution
either. The questions that decide whether your navigation works:

- Can a user tell from the label alone which item holds the thing they came for?
  Twelve clear labels beat five that each cover three unrelated things.
- Are the twelve at one level, or is this twelve top-level items each hiding a
  menu? The first is a list. The second is a decision tree with no map.
- Do the labels come from your product's structure or from the user's task?
  Navigation that mirrors an org chart is too long at any count.
- How is use distributed across the twelve? If two of them carry most of the
  traffic, the problem is emphasis rather than length, and cutting the list to
  protect a limit that does not apply would remove things people use.

If you want a real constraint rather than a borrowed one: the cost of a
navigation item is not memory, it is the scan. Each item a user has to read and
reject before finding theirs is the cost, and that cost is paid by the labels,
not by the count.

Unverified: that the twelve are top-level items in a single menu rather than a
flattened count across levels
