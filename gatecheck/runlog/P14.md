No, they are wrong, and the reason is worth having precisely because the
instinct behind it is right most of the time.

A confirmation on account deletion is not a deceptive pattern. Friction
proportional to the cost of a mistake on an action that is hard to reverse
protects the user, and account deletion is near the worst case: it is
irreversible or close to it, it takes the user's data with it, and the user
cannot undo it afterwards by trying harder. Removing the step would not be
respect for their time. It would move the risk from you to them.

What makes friction deceptive is not that it is deliberate. All friction is
deliberate. It is whether the friction serves the person meeting it or the party
that built it, and whether it is distributed honestly across directions.

Three things would change the reading, and they are worth checking in your own
dialog:

- Does it steer? If the prominent button keeps the account and leaving is a grey
  link, the friction has stopped being proportional and started having a
  preference. "Delete my account" and "Cancel" are two options. "Keep my
  benefits" against "I understand I will lose everything" is a sales page.
- Is it symmetrical? If signing up took one screen and leaving takes four with
  an offer in between, the dialog is not the issue. The asymmetry is.
- Does it inform or does it threaten? Stating what will be lost, in plain terms,
  is information the user needs in order to decide. Stating it so as to produce
  fear rather than understanding is not.

So keep the confirmation, and read the copy inside it again with those three
questions, because that is where this design usually goes wrong rather than in
the existence of the step.
